# Spatial Blocking and Physics

## Establish the first frame

State only the spatial facts needed to read the action:

- Occupancy: who and what are already present.
- Screen or world position: left, center, right, foreground, midground, background, or a named place.
- Facing and gaze: which person, object, path, or landmark receives attention.
- Landmarks: doors, windows, vehicles, furniture, edges, or terrain that anchor movement.
- Contact: what each hand, foot, body, prop, liquid, or surface is touching or supporting.

Prefer relational descriptions such as `beside the doorway` or `behind the stalled car` over arbitrary measurements. Omit a category when it does not affect the shot.

## Preserve direction and state

Across numbered shots, keep established screen direction, entrances, exits, prop ownership, contact, damage, wetness, and character state until an on-screen action changes them. If a cut reverses the viewing axis intentionally, establish the new orientation with a landmark or visible reorientation rather than silently flipping positions.

For a head-on encounter, establish opposite starting ends and opposing travel vectors before assigning screen-left or screen-right lanes. Carry those opposing vectors through each cut until the characters pass; parallel movement toward the same camera cannot produce a meeting.

Bind those vectors to the camera orientation: use screen-left and screen-right travel only with a clearly perpendicular side-on view; when the camera looks down the corridor length, describe motion as toward or away in depth instead.

## Select physical cues

Describe physical cause and visible consequence only where they support the main event:

- Mass and inertia: delayed starts, continued travel, recoil, or recovery.
- Contact and friction: grip, slip, drag, compression, scraping, or resistance.
- Weight transfer: planted feet, a shifted center of mass, a stumble, or a brace.
- Cloth: lag, stretch, fold, flutter, cling, or settling caused by motion, wind, or water.
- Liquid: flow direction, splash, droplets, pooling, adhesion, or runoff caused by gravity and contact.
- Debris and impact: a clear point of contact followed by displacement, fracture, dust, rebound, or settling.

Use the smallest set of cues that makes the event coherent. Keep each shot centered on one dominant visual event.

## Stylized worlds

Respect explicitly surreal, dreamlike, weightless, elastic, miniature, stop-motion, or otherwise stylized physics. Define the requested rule and apply it consistently within the shot instead of forcing ordinary realism. Preserve causal readability even when the cause is fantastical.

## Exclusions

- Do not invent fixed distances, field-of-view degrees, lens rituals, exposure settings, or technical camera measurements.
- Do not measure every movement or prescribe unnecessary trajectories.
- Do not add collisions, particles, cloth motion, liquid behavior, or debris when they are irrelevant to the requested event.
- Do not guarantee physically perfect simulation or exact continuity from the model.
